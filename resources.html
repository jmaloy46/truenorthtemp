Resources & Tips

Everything you need to help a rescue dog settle in, thrive, and feel at home — from the first 72 hours to lifelong care.

First things first

Bringing Your New Dog Home

Rescue dogs have often had a long journey to New York. A calm, predictable start sets everyone up for success.

🧭

Go slow & keep it calm

Skip the welcome party. Give your dog a quiet space to retreat to, and let them explore one room at a time on their own schedule.

⏰

Build a routine

Predictable meals, walks, and bedtime help a nervous dog learn that they're safe. Routine is the fastest way to build trust.

🦮

Leash & ID from day one

Use a secure harness with tags on every outing, even in the yard. Scared dogs can bolt — your microchip is registered, so keep it updated too.

The decompression rule

The 3-3-3 Rule

A simple way to picture how most rescue dogs adjust. Every dog is different — let yours set the pace.

3

First 3 days

Overwhelmed and decompressing. Your dog may hide, sleep a lot, or not eat much. Keep things quiet and give them room to breathe.

3

First 3 weeks

Settling in. They start to learn your routine, test a few boundaries, and let their real personality begin to show.

3

First 3 months

Feeling at home. Trust is built, comfort sets in, and your dog truly understands that this is their forever family.

Before pickup day

New-Dog Home Checklist

Whether you're adopting or fostering, a little prep goes a long way. Have these ready before your dog walks through the door.

  • A crate or cozy bed in a quiet, low-traffic corner
  • Food & water bowls (ask your foster what food they've been eating to avoid tummy upset)
  • A properly fitted harness, leash, and ID tags
  • Puppy pads or a potty plan for the first few days
  • A few simple chew toys and an enrichment puzzle
  • Enzyme cleaner for inevitable accidents
  • Baby gates to limit access while they settle
  • The name and number of a local vet

Set up for success

Training & Vet Care

Positive habits and good health go hand in hand. These are the basics we recommend for every True North dog.

🎾

Positive reinforcement

Reward the behavior you want with treats, praise, and play. Keep sessions short and upbeat — never punish a fearful dog.

👥

Gentle socialization

Introduce new people, dogs, and sounds slowly and at a distance your dog is comfortable with. Let them choose to approach.

🩺

Stay on top of vetting

Every TNR dog leaves fully vetted, microchipped, and fixed. Keep up annual checkups, vaccines, and monthly flea, tick & heartworm prevention.

Good to know

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions from adopters and fosters. Still stuck? We're always happy to help.

How does fostering work if there's no shelter?

True North is 100% foster-based — we have no physical shelter, so every dog we save lives in a volunteer's home. We cover approved vet costs and provide guidance; you provide the love, the couch space, and the daily care until the dog is adopted.

What does fostering cost me?

True North covers veterinary care for dogs in our program. Fosters typically provide food, basic supplies, and lots of patience — but if cost is a barrier, just ask. We never want money to stop someone from saving a life.

How long does a foster commitment last?

It varies by dog. Some are adopted within a couple of weeks; others need a few months to find the right match. We'll always talk through the expected timeline with you, and short-term and weekend fosters are welcome too.

Can I foster if I already have pets or kids?

Absolutely — many of our best fosters do. We match dogs to your household, so tell us about your resident pets and family on your application and we'll find a good fit. Slow, supervised introductions are key.

What's included in the adoption fee?

Every adoptable dog is spayed or neutered, up to date on age-appropriate vaccines, microchipped, and vet-checked before going home. Adoption fees and donations are what fund the rescue, transport, and medical care of the next dog in need.

My new dog seems scared or won't eat. Is that normal?

Very often, yes — especially in the first few days (see the 3-3-3 rule above). Give them a quiet space, a steady routine, and time. If a dog won't eat or drink for more than 24–48 hours, seems ill, or anything worries you, reach out to your vet and let us know.

Where do True North dogs come from?

We partner with rescues in Texas, North Carolina, and Puerto Rico, and we're proud to be the only East Coast partner for Slaughterhouse Survivors in Harbin, China, rescuing dogs from the meat trade and bringing them to loving homes in New York.
